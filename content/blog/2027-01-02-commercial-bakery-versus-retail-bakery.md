Say the word bakery and most people picture a shop with a glass case, a counter and the smell of fresh bread. That picture is accurate for some bakeries. Others look nothing like it. They are production plants that make bread, rolls and pastries in volume and send them out to be sold somewhere else. Both are bakeries, and the industry has separate names for them.

This lesson explains how the two types are defined, what each one makes, and why the difference matters if you run a bakery, plan to start one, or buy equipment for one.

## Why the industry splits bakeries into two groups

Governments and researchers group businesses by what they mainly do. In North America this is done with NAICS codes, short for the North American Industry Classification System. Each code describes one kind of business, so that statistics, reports and market studies compare similar businesses with each other.

Bakeries get two codes that sit side by side. Commercial bakeries are code 311812. Retail bakeries are code 311811. The codes are close together because the work is close together. The difference is in who the product is made for and where it is sold.

:::note What the codes mean
NAICS 311811 covers retail bakeries, and NAICS 311812 covers commercial bakeries. Each code has its own definition, and the two definitions exclude each other.
:::

## What a retail bakery is

A retail bakery, under NAICS 311811, makes bread and other baked goods on its own premises, starting from flour rather than from prepared dough. It then sells those products at retail, for customers to eat away from the store.

Three details in that definition matter.

First, the baking happens where the product is sold. The oven and the counter are in the same building.

Second, the baker starts from flour. A shop that buys prepared dough and only bakes it off does not meet the definition.

Third, the product is sold for consumption away from the store. A business that mainly serves food to be eaten on site belongs to a different category.

## What a commercial bakery is

A commercial bakery, under NAICS 311812, is a manufacturer. The code covers businesses mainly engaged in making fresh and frozen bread and bread-type rolls, along with other fresh bakery products. Cookies and crackers are excluded.

The examples listed under the code show how wide it is: breads, rolls, buns and bagels, fresh pastries, croissants, cakes, doughnuts and pies, soft pretzels, biscuits, croutons and bread crumbs, matzo and unleavened bread, communion wafers, sweet yeast goods and knishes.

The definition does not say how commercial bakeries sell what they make. That is worth noticing. A commercial bakery is defined by manufacturing, not by a particular sales channel.

:::note The retail code excludes wholesale bakers
The retail bakery definition specifically excludes bakeries that make bread or baked goods for wholesale. Those fall under the commercial bakery code.
:::

## A third category: selling what someone else baked

Not every business that sells baked goods is a bakery in this sense. Retailing bakery products that were made elsewhere falls under a separate code, NAICS 445291, baked goods stores. Businesses that sell bakery items for immediate eating fall under yet another code, NAICS 722515.

This is useful when you read market data. If a report counts only commercial bakeries, it is not counting the shop down the street. If it counts retail bakeries, it is not counting the plant that supplies the grocery store.

## Why the difference matters for equipment

The definitions are about classification, but they point to real differences in how a bakery has to be set up. A bakery that manufactures bread and rolls to be sold elsewhere has to think about how product moves through the building: out of the oven, onto cooling, into transport. A bakery that sells from a counter on the same premises has to think about how product gets from the oven to the display.

Either way, product has to be handled on racks, in baskets and on carts, and that handling equipment gets used every day. It is worth choosing it for the job rather than buying whatever is closest.

## Questions to ask before you plan a purchase

Whether your bakery is a shop or a plant, a few questions will keep you from buying the wrong thing.

Where does product go after it leaves the oven? Map the path, step by step, from the oven to the cooling area to the customer or the truck. Equipment should fit that path, not the other way around.

What has to move together? Racks and carts that nest, stack or roll through the same doorways save time. Measure the doorways and aisles before you order.

What conditions will the equipment face? Some racks go into ovens or blast freezers, and some will be washed often. Finish and material should match the conditions.

Do you need something custom? Standard sizes work for many bakeries. When they do not, a fabricator can build to your measurements.

## The short version

A retail bakery makes baked goods from flour on its own premises and sells them to customers who take them away. A commercial bakery manufactures bread and other fresh bakery products as its main business, and the definition does not tie it to a retail counter. A store that sells goods baked somewhere else is neither one.

Knowing which type you are, or which type you supply, makes it easier to read industry data, talk to suppliers and plan the equipment that keeps product moving.
