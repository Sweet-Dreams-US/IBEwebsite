Bread and rolls made in a commercial bakery follow the same basic steps as bread made at home. The difference is volume. When a plant turns out thousands of loaves or buns an hour, every step has to be repeatable, and every piece of equipment between the mixer and the wrapper has to keep the product moving. This article walks through those steps and explains what changes when the product is sold fresh or frozen.

## What counts as a commercial bakery

Industry classification gives a useful starting point. In the North American Industry Classification System, code 311812 covers commercial bakeries. IBISWorld, which publishes a summary of that code, describes the industry as establishments "primarily engaged in manufacturing fresh and frozen bread and bread-type rolls." The code also covers other fresh bakery products, and it excludes cookies and crackers.

The code's examples include rolls and buns made in commercial bakeries, including frozen ones. A separate code, 311811, covers retail bakeries, which is the category for shops that bake and sell to customers directly. The split is a reminder that a bakery selling over a counter and a bakery supplying grocery stores and restaurants are running different kinds of operations.

:::note Worth knowing
NAICS 311812 covers "fresh and frozen bread and bread-type rolls" and lists rolls and buns, including frozen, among its examples. Cookies and crackers are excluded.
:::

## The four steps every bread process shares

The Federation of Bakers, a trade body in the United Kingdom, says that "All bread making processes rely on four key steps": mixing, proving or fermenting, baking, and cooling. Large bakeries add dividing, slicing, and packaging around those four, but the core does not change from one process to the next.

The same source says that plant bread production takes around four hours from end to end. That number gives you a sense of the pace. Dough that is mixed in the morning can be on a shelf by the afternoon, so each stage has to hand off to the next without delay.

## From mixing to the oven

The University of Michigan's chemical engineering encyclopedia describes the early stages in order. Dry ingredients go into the mixer, followed by water and yeast, until a dough forms. Gluten gives the dough its elasticity and structure.

Next comes fermentation. Yeast converts sugars into ethanol and carbon dioxide, which aerates the dough. The source gives the usual temperature for bulk fermentation as between 21 and 37 degrees Celsius, or 70 to 100 degrees Fahrenheit, and says it can last an hour or more. It also describes a sponge method, in which a portion of the dough ferments for up to five hours, and the Chorleywood process, which uses high-speed mixing to skip the first fermentation. The Federation of Bakers says large bakeries mostly use the Chorleywood process.

After fermentation, the dough is divided into pieces, each of which becomes one loaf, and moved along a conveyor. A short rest, called proofing, lets the dough pieces rise to the volume the baker wants before they go into the oven. The same source gives oven settings of 180 to 230 degrees Celsius, or 350 to 450 degrees Fahrenheit, with baking times from 15 minutes to over an hour depending on the product.

## Why cooling gets so much attention

Cooling is the stage people tend to overlook, and it matters more than it looks. According to the University of Michigan source, bread cools on racks or a conveyor until it reaches room temperature. Humidity is monitored during this stage because, in the source's words, "Too much moisture could lead to mold formation."

Slicing and wrapping come after cooling, so every loaf or bun has to be handled in a way that lets it cool evenly. This is where racks come in. A cooling rack holds the product at a working height, keeps air moving around it, and lets staff move many pieces at once.

## What changes for frozen products

NAICS puts fresh and frozen bread and rolls in the same industry, so a plant may make both. The sources used for this article describe the fresh bread process in detail and do not go into freezing, so this article does not cover how freezing is done. What is clear is that a frozen product has an added step after baking and cooling, and that it uses equipment built for that step.

Industrial Bakery Equipment lists Blast Freeze / Drying Racks among its products, along with a spec sheet on blast freezer racks. If your plant freezes product, rack size, load capacity, and the way racks move through the freezer are worth discussing with an equipment supplier before you buy.

## Moving dough and product between stages

Between mixing and packaging, dough and finished product are handled many times. Dough has to be held and moved before it is divided. Baked product has to move from the oven to cooling, from cooling to the slicer, and from the slicer to the wrapper.

Industrial Bakery Equipment lists several kinds of equipment for these moves, including dough troughs in several body styles (Control Flow, Sloping Bottom, and Drop-Side), pan tree mobile racks, bread transport and display racks, and stainless steel high density mobile racks. Which ones suit a bakery depends on its product, its floor layout, and how often product changes.

## Questions to settle before you buy equipment

If you are planning or expanding a bread or roll line, it helps to answer a few questions first:

1. What products will you make, and how many pieces per hour?
2. Will the product be sold fresh, frozen, or both?
3. How much floor space is there between the oven, the cooler, and the slicer?
4. Does the equipment need to be mobile, or can it stay in one place?
5. What finish and material suit your sanitation routine?

Having these answers written down makes a conversation with a supplier faster and gives you a better chance of getting equipment that fits the first time.
