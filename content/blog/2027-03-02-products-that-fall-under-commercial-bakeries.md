Ask ten people what a commercial bakery is and you may hear ten answers. One person pictures a shop with a display case. Another pictures a plant that makes bread for grocery stores. A third thinks of a facility that turns out doughnuts or bagels by the thousand. All of these are bakeries, but they are not the same kind of business, and the industry codes used to sort businesses treat them differently.

This article looks at the code for commercial bakeries, which is NAICS 311812, and at the products it names. If you work in food production, buy equipment for a bakery, or are only trying to understand the trade, the list is a useful place to start.

## Why the label matters

A business needs a name for what it does. When a company fills out a form, applies for a listing, or is counted in a report, it is placed under an industry code. The code is short, but it carries a definition. Two bakeries can both make bread and still land under different codes, because the codes separate making a product from selling it.

That difference shapes how people talk about the work. A commercial bakery is described by what it manufactures. A retail bakery is described by what it sells to the public. Keeping the two apart makes it easier to see what each kind of operation needs, from floor space to the equipment that moves product from one step to the next.

## What the code says

The commercial bakeries code covers establishments manufacturing fresh and frozen bread and bread-type rolls and other fresh bakery products, with cookies and crackers excluded. That one sentence holds three ideas.

First, the code includes both fresh and frozen bread. A plant does not have to sell its loaves warm to fall under this code. Second, the phrase "bread-type rolls" brings in rolls and buns alongside loaves. Third, the code reaches past bread to other fresh bakery products, and it carves out cookies and crackers, which are classified elsewhere.

:::note What the code covers
NAICS 311812 covers establishments manufacturing fresh and frozen bread and bread-type rolls and other fresh bakery products, except cookies and crackers.
:::

## The products named in the list

The code does not stop at a general description. It lists examples, and the examples show how wide the category is. They can be grouped by the kind of product.

Breads and rolls come first. The list names frozen bread and bread-type rolls, bread-type biscuits, and unleavened bread and matzo. Bagels and soft pretzels are on the list as well.

Pastries and sweet goods make up the next group. The list names fresh pastries such as Danish and French pastries, croissants, and sweet yeast goods that are not frozen. Doughnuts and crullers appear with the same limit, which is that frozen ones are excluded from this code.

Cakes and pies follow. Cakes that are not frozen are named, and so are fresh pies.

The last group is a mix of smaller products. Croutons and bread crumbs are on the list. So are knishes that are not frozen, and communion wafers.

The "except frozen" notes on several items are worth reading closely. They show that the code makes its own distinctions even inside one product type. A fresh cake and a frozen cake are not placed in the same spot.

## What the list leaves out

The definition says plainly that cookies and crackers are not part of this code. Many people expect them to be, since cookies and crackers are baked goods. They are classified separately, so a plant that makes only cookies and crackers would not be described by the commercial bakeries code.

This is a good example of why the list is worth reading. A product being baked does not decide the code. The code decides by the specific products named and by whether the business manufactures them.

## How the retail code differs

The neighboring code is NAICS 311811, retail bakeries. It covers shops that sell bread and other baked goods for customers to take away rather than eat on site. These bakeries mix and bake from flour on their own premises instead of using premade dough.

Put side by side, the difference is clear. The commercial bakeries code is about manufacturing fresh and frozen bakery products. The retail bakeries code is about baking from flour on the premises and selling to customers who take the product with them. The same source points to other related codes too. One covers stores that sell baked goods made elsewhere. Another covers businesses that prepare and sell bakery items for immediate consumption.

:::note Retail is a separate code
The source describes retail bakeries as baking from flour on the premises and retailing the product for customers to take away, which is a different activity from the manufacturing described in the commercial bakeries code.
:::

## Reading the list as a planning tool

If you are planning a bakery operation, the product list can help you ask better questions. Each item on it has to be made, cooled, held and moved. A plant that makes bread has to get the product from the oven to packaging. A plant that makes doughnuts has to handle a very different shape. A plant that makes pies has to protect a product that is easily damaged.

Whatever the product, a bakery has to move it between steps, and that means racks, carts, baskets and containers. The list of products is also, in a rough way, a list of handling problems.

## Putting it together

The commercial bakeries code is built around manufacturing. Its definition covers fresh and frozen bread and bread-type rolls and other fresh bakery products, and it names a wide range of examples, from croissants and bagels to cakes, pies, croutons and communion wafers. It leaves out cookies and crackers, and it sits apart from the retail bakeries code, which describes baking from flour on the premises for customers who take the product with them.

Knowing where those lines fall helps when you are describing a business, reading an industry report, or choosing equipment for a bakery. Start with the product, decide whether the business manufactures it or sells it, and the right label follows.
