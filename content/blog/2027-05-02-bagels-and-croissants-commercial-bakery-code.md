If you run a commercial bakery, or plan to, you will see the industry code for your type of business sooner or later. It shows up on forms, in market reports and in equipment and insurance paperwork. The code also names the products it covers, and two of them are bagels and croissants.

This article explains what that code says, how bagels and croissants differ in the way they are made, and what that means for the equipment on your floor.

## What the code says

The North American Industry Classification System, or NAICS, groups businesses by what they mainly do. Commercial bakeries fall under code 311812. IBISWorld describes it as covering establishments primarily engaged in manufacturing fresh and frozen bread and bread-type rolls and other fresh bakery products, except cookies and crackers.

The business examples listed under that code include bagels, croissants, Danish and French pastries when fresh, doughnuts except frozen, baked cakes except frozen, fresh pies, soft pretzels, rolls and buns including frozen, croutons and bread crumbs, matzo and unleavened bread, and communion wafers.

:::note Worth knowing
IBISWorld lists bagels and croissants as examples of products made by commercial bakeries under NAICS 311812.
:::

## How it differs from a retail bakery

Retail bakeries have their own code, 311811. According to IBISWorld, that code is for retailing bread and other bakery products, not for immediate consumption, made on the premises from flour and not from prepared dough. The key difference is that 311812 covers manufacturing, while 311811 covers retail sales of goods baked on site from flour.

For a bakery owner, the practical point is volume and flow. A manufacturing operation moves product through mixing, forming, baking and cooling in repeated batches, and the equipment has to hold up to that pace.

## How a bagel is made

Bakeinfo says bagels are rings of yeast dough that are boiled or poached before baking. That step is what makes them different from bread. Boiling gelatinises the starch on the surface of the dough, which gives a glossy exterior. It also sets the outside structure so the bagel keeps its shape in the oven.

Bakeinfo adds that some products skip boiling and use heavy steam or an egg wash for gloss instead. It says these save time and labour, and it calls them crusty rolls and not bagels.

So a bagel line has a step that a bread line does not: the water. Whatever holds the bagels during that step has to work with it.

## How a croissant is made

The American Society of Baking describes the croissant as a straight dough that is mixed, fermented, and then refrigerated for 8 to 24 hours at 4 degrees C (40 degrees F). Fat is rolled into the dough in layers. The standard amount of roll-in fat is 25 percent of total dough weight. The dough is sheeted and laminated, then folded. The page says three three-folds produce 54 layers, and the dough rests in the refrigerator for 30 minutes between folds.

The sheeted dough is then cut into triangles and rolled into a crescent. The pieces proof for 90 to 120 minutes at 26 degrees C (79 degrees F) and 75 to 80 percent relative humidity. The page notes that proofing temperature should never exceed the melting point of the fat. The croissants are egg washed and baked for 10 to 20 minutes at 200 degrees C (392 degrees F).

:::note Worth knowing
The American Society of Baking says croissant dough rests in the refrigerator for 30 minutes between folds, and shaped pieces proof for 90 to 120 minutes.
:::

## What this means for the floor

Put the two products side by side and the difference is clear. A bagel line is built around forming, boiling and baking. A croissant line is built around cold rests, careful temperature control and long proofing. Both need space to hold product between stages, and both need racks and carts that suit the stage the product is in.

That is why the layout of a bakery matters as much as the ovens. Product that waits for a rest, a proof or a bake needs a place to wait that does not slow the next step. Racks, baskets, troughs and carts are the tools that carry that work.
