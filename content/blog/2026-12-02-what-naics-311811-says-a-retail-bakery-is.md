Anyone who works around bakeries eventually runs into a six digit number. It shows up in business filings, trade statistics, insurance forms and market reports. The number for retail bakeries is 311811, and the definition behind it is more specific than most people expect. Knowing what it says helps you understand where a retail bakery ends and a larger production bakery begins, and that difference shapes the equipment each one needs.

## What the code is and what 311811 covers

The code comes from NAICS, the North American Industry Classification System. Each code groups businesses by the main activity they do. For 311811, the title is Retail Bakeries, and the definition, as summarized by IBISWorld, says the code covers establishments primarily engaged in retailing bread and other bakery products not for immediate consumption.

Two ideas sit inside that one sentence. The first is that the business sells to the public, which is what retailing means here. The second is that the products are not meant to be eaten right away. A loaf you take home, a box of rolls for dinner, or a cake you pick up for a party all fit that description. A pastry eaten at the counter is a different kind of sale.

## Made on the premises from flour

The definition adds a condition about how the product is made. The goods have to be made on the premises from flour, not from prepared dough. IBISWorld lists a single example for the code: bakeries with baking from flour on the premises, retailing not for immediate consumption.

This is the part that most often surprises people. A shop that receives frozen or prepared dough and only bakes it off is not described by this definition. The code is aimed at the bakery that starts with flour, mixes its own dough and bakes what it sells.

:::

## What "not for immediate consumption" means in practice

The phrase draws a line between a bakery and a place that serves food to be eaten on the spot. The bakery the code describes sells take-home goods. That is a useful way to picture the operation: product is baked in batches, cooled, stored or displayed, and then carried out the door by customers.

Even without any other detail, that picture tells you what the back of the shop has to handle. Dough has to be mixed and held somewhere. Baked goods have to cool before they are bagged or boxed. Finished product has to be moved from the oven area to the sales area. Each step needs space and the right racks, trays and carts.

## How it differs from a commercial bakery

The neighboring code is 311812, Commercial Bakeries. IBISWorld describes it as covering establishments primarily engaged in manufacturing fresh and frozen bread and bread type rolls and other fresh bakery products, except cookies and crackers. Cookies and crackers fall under a separate code, 311821.

The split is between retailing and manufacturing. The 311812 examples include fresh pastries such as Danish and French pastries and croissants, frozen bread, rolls and buns, bagels, soft pretzels and doughnuts that are not frozen, fresh cakes and pies, croutons and bread crumbs, and unleavened bread such as matzo.

IBISWorld says a bakery that makes and sells its own bread on the premises belongs in 311811, while operations built around production belong in 311812.

:::note Worth knowing
IBISWorld's summary of NAICS 311812 describes commercial bakeries as manufacturers of fresh and frozen bread and rolls and other fresh bakery products, except cookies and crackers.
:::

## Where a shop that sells other people's baked goods fits

There is a third case. IBISWorld notes that businesses selling baked goods made elsewhere fall under 445291, Baked Goods Stores. So the three codes sort the field by what the business really does. One bakes from flour and sells to the public. One manufactures bakery products. One sells products baked somewhere else.

If you are reading an industry report, filling out a form or comparing your shop to others, check which code applies. Numbers for retail bakeries and commercial bakeries describe different kinds of operations, and mixing them gives a misleading comparison.

## What the definition means for your equipment list

A definition is not an equipment plan, but it points to the questions worth asking. A bakery that works from flour makes its own dough, so it needs a way to hold and move dough. It bakes in batches, so it needs somewhere to cool product and a way to carry it. It sells take-home goods, so it needs racks or carts that get bread from the baking area to the display or packaging area.

A commercial bakery makes the same kinds of products at larger volume. The equipment questions are similar, but the quantities change. More dough means larger containers. More product means more cooling capacity and more transport racks.
